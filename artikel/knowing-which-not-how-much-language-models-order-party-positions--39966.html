Knowing Which, Not How Much: Language Models Order Party Positions They Cannot Place

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 18 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (228 words, 📖 Standard)

arXiv:2611.21878v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Language models are increasingly asked where real actors stand: what a party's platform prioritises, what an institution has committed to. Such judgments are gradable, carrying both an order and a magnitude. Nearly every benchmark that tests them asks about one entity at a time, then reads that score as the model's knowledge of the domain. Order and magnitude, however, need not be acquired together. Text about social entities carries far more comparison than measurement, so a model may hold a dependable ordering with no anchored scale beneath it. We ask whether a model can place a documented social position on a scale or only order the actors it compares, and what that means for how such knowledge is measured. We test this on party positions in Japan and South Korea, where the Comparative Manifesto Project's coded data supplies a rare external answer key. Asked to order four parties, models beat the strongest fixed-answer baseline by more than a factor of two; asked to place one party on the same scale, they fall below it. The ordering survives inside those failed placements, and supplying the coded values lifts placement to near ceiling. Comparative and absolute questions about the same fact therefore measure different things here, and for party problem salience a benchmark built from one cannot speak for the other.
