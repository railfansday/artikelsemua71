TurboWAM: Learning What Matters for Fast World Action Models

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (246 words, 📖 Standard)

Industry observers note that arXiv:2610.33941v3 Announce Type: replace Abstract: World action models have demonstrated promising performance in robot policy learning, yet their high inference latency remains a critical bottleneck for real-world deployment. Recent approaches have sought to improve inference efficiency by eliminating test-time future-frame imagination or accelerating action prediction. However, aggregating representations from all video world model layers can introduce redundant computation, while standard backpropagation may distribute action-supervision gradients unevenly across layers, limiting the effectiveness of world representation learning. In this work, we propose TurboWAM, a fast world action model that learns to efficiently aggregate and optimize world representations for action prediction. Specifically, TurboWAM introduces an efficient world representation aggregation mechanism that restricts aggregation to a bounded range of relevant world model layers identified through layer relevance analysis. Within this bounded range, TurboWAM estimates aggregation weights for different world model layers based on the current action denoising state and combines them with learnable layer-wise weights to fuse world representations. Moreover, TurboWAM employs a world model gradient modulation mechanism that decomposes action-supervision gradients into a layer-shared component and layer-specific residual components, enabling more balanced gradient propagation across world model layers. This mechanism operates exclusively during training and introduces no additional inference computation. Together, these designs enable more efficient world representation learning and low-latency action prediction. Extensive experiments on multiple robot manipulation benchmarks and real-world manipulation tasks show that TurboWAM achieves low inference latency while maintaining competitive task success rates compared with state-of-the-art methods.
