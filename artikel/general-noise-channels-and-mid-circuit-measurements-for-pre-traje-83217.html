General Noise Channels and Mid-Circuit Measurements for Pre-Trajectory Sampling with Batched Execution

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (284 words, 📖 Standard)

Recent findings suggest that arXiv:2611.23923v1 Announce Type: fresh Abstract: Many studies of quantum protocols require fast sampling of large amounts of measurement data from noisy quantum circuits, such as for training machine-learning decoders for quantum error correction. Exact simulation of realistic noise is expensive, while stabilizer-based simulators are fast but approximate, for example through Pauli twirling, which generally alters the output statistics. Pre-trajectory sampling with batched execution (PTSBE) accelerates exact trajectory simulation by sampling noise before state evolution. This method requires the outcome probability of every stochastic event to be independent of the state, which excludes mid-circuit measurements and general Kraus channels. We present a generalization of PTSBE to measurement and Kraus operators without approximation. Each event is partially pre-sampled, while deferred outcomes are drawn during grouped execution via multinomial branching and cached-state reuse. We implement the method for GPUs as an extension of CUDA-Q. On distance-3 topological code memories under amplitude damping, it is up to 24x faster than conventional trajectory sampling in CUDA-Q. On 30-qubit random circuits with mid-circuit measurements, it is up to 83x faster and outperforms the near-Clifford simulator Clifft when more than about 25% of the gates are non-Clifford. With repeated sampling of each final state, its sampling rate per trajectory exceeds that of CUDA-Q by nearly five orders of magnitude. Our method also allows us to compare exact amplitude damping with its Pauli-twirled channel on a surface-code memory, revealing that twirling overestimates the detection rate by 21% and raises the logical error rate of a lookup decoder by 8%. This work makes exact, high-throughput simulation practical for general noisy circuits, from data generation for training decoders to studying measurement-based fault-tolerant protocols under realistic noise.
