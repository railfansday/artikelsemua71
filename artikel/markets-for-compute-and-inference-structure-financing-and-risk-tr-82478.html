Markets for Compute and Inference: Structure, Financing, and Risk Transfer

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 19 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 2 min read (314 words, 📖 Standard)

Sources indicate that arXiv:2611.20242v2 Announce Type: replace Abstract: Capacity to run large language models is rented by the accelerator-hour, and their output is sold by the million tokens. Both goods now have published price indices, an over-the-counter forward has traded on one, compute futures are listed offshore, and CME and ICE have specified futures on rental indices that await the United States regulator. We ask what in these two markets can be hedged, by whom, and at what price. Three findings stand out. First, the price that exchange contracts settle on is not the price that drives operators' revenue. The contracts on CME and ICE reference published short-dated rental rates, while most revenue, and with it credit quality, comes from private multi-year contracts whose prices are largely undisclosed. Second, the token price and the rental rate are linked by an identity through throughput and margin, but they are different exposures, so a hedge on the rental rate does not hedge the cost of inference; at the measured capability of GPT-5 the cheapest list price fell by a factor of 17 in twelve months, and by far less at lower capability. Third, the demand for hedging already sits in credit agreements, as the years by which operators' debt outlives their contracted revenue. Filings cannot measure that gap, because debt is scheduled year by year while contracted revenue rarely is, and the one loan that prices it settles the risk by cash prepayment against a model only the parties see. We design a reverse convertible note through which leveraged operators could buy protection against falling rental rates. Its coupon depends steeply on the annualised volatility of the rental rate, which the two public indices put between about 5% and 89% according to how each is built and the horizon measured; across that range the fair one-year coupon runs from 4.1% to 31.6%.
