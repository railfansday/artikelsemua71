Breaking: A Little Advice Suffices: Diffie-Hellman Is as Hard as Discrete Logarithm

🌐 Source: Arxiv • 📅 18 Nov 2026 • ⏱️ 1 min read (247 words, 📖 Standard)

Recent findings suggest that arXiv:2611.22290v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Is computing a Diffie-Hellman key as hard as computing a discrete logarithm? Maurer showed that it is, given a few bits of advice, provided that short intervals contain enough integers with only small prime factors; that proviso has stood for thirty years. We remove it. For every prime $p$ there is an advice string of $O(\log p)$ bits, depending only on $p$, with which discrete logarithms in every group of order $p$ are computed in polynomial time from a Diffie-Hellman oracle. The reduction is the classical one; what was missing was a proof that short intervals contain polylogarithmically smooth integers. We show that a zero-free half-plane for the Riemann zeta function, which is now known, gives the expected number of $(\log x)^K$-smooth integers in every interval $[x,x+x^\theta]$ with $\theta>17/30$. With smoothness this small the saddle point of the counting integral lies just to the left of the line $\mathrm{Re}\,s=1$, and a zero-free strip of any fixed width absorbs the shift once $K$ is large. Genus-2 Jacobians, whose orders fill such intervals, then turn one of these integers into the order of an auxiliary group. The same input removes the smoothness assumption from Maurer and Raub's impossibility of field-homomorphic one-way permutations and from the yes/no-query reduction of Koblitz, Menezes and Shparlinski. It also makes Wolf's characterisation unconditional: discrete logarithm and Diffie-Hellman are generically equivalent exactly when the group order has no large repeated prime factor.
